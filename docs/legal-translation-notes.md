# Legal translation notes

Written while translating the terms of service, privacy notice and cancellation
policy from English into Georgian and Russian.

Every item below is either a terminology decision a lawyer should confirm, or a
place where the ENGLISH is ambiguous and the translation had to choose a
reading. Several are worth reading even if the translations are thrown away,
because they are questions about the English.

The English governs. These notes exist so that a review covers the choices
rather than rediscovering them.

## Georgian

- Placeholders kept verbatim as ${COMPANY}, ${ENTITY}, ${ENTITY_ID}, ${ENTITY_ADDRESS}, ${CONTACT} so they splice into the same template literals in src/lib/legal.ts. Georgian inflects names by case, so sentences were restructured to keep the placeholders in the nominative: the ID and address are moved into a parenthetical, 'Notices in writing go to ...' becomes 'იგზავნება მისამართზე: ${ENTITY}, ${ENTITY_ADDRESS}', and 'we\' means ${ENTITY}' becomes 'ნიშნავს კომპანიას ${ENTITY}' where the case-carrying word is the added noun კომპანიას. ${CONTACT} takes the hyphenated suffix '-ზე' (the convention already used in ka.ts for Latin-script placeholders); please confirm the rendered email address reads acceptably with that hyphen.
- Address form: the site consistently uses second person singular (შენ) with travellers, so the translation does too for direct address ('შენი ჯავშანი', 'მოგვწერე'), while obligations, definitions and liability wording stay impersonal. Georgian legal drafting conventionally uses თქვენ or a fully impersonal register in a binding contract; please confirm the singular is acceptable for terms that form a contract, and note that switching would require touching every sentence, not just pronouns.
- 'გთხოვთ' in the cancellation-notice sentences is 'we ask you' (the -თ marks the plural subject 'we'), not a polite plural addressed to the reader; it was chosen over an imperative precisely to keep the 24-hour notice a request rather than an obligation, matching the English 'We ask for'.
- 'carrier' → 'გადამზიდველი', the Civil Code term for the party to a contract of carriage. The alternative 'გადამყვანი' is closer to passenger carriage in everyday use. The sentence 'We are not the carrier' is rendered flatly as 'ჩვენ არ ვართ გადამზიდველი' with no hedging; please confirm the term choice, since this is the load-bearing exclusion in the document.
- 'marketplace' → 'პლატფორმა'. Georgian has no settled legal term; 'მარკეტფლეისი' is a transliteration and 'ონლაინ პლატფორმა' is the nearest regulatory phrase. 'პლატფორმა' was chosen because the rest of the site already uses it.
- 'independent operators' → 'დამოუკიდებელი შემსრულებლები'. There is no clean Georgian equivalent. 'დამოუკიდებელი მეწარმეები' would have asserted registered-entrepreneur status that the English does not, and 'გადამზიდველები' would have duplicated the carrier term. If the intended meaning is that each driver is a separately registered business, the wording should be strengthened deliberately rather than left as it is.
- 'hold the agreed price' is ambiguous in the English — it could mean keeping the price fixed or holding funds. It is translated as keeping the price unchanged ('უცვლელად ვინარჩუნებთ შეთანხმებულ ფასს'), consistent with the Prices section. If it was meant to describe an authorisation hold on a card, the English and the Georgian both need rewriting.
- 'our insurance requirements' is ambiguous: requirements we impose on drivers, or insurance cover we provide. Translated as requirements we set ('ჩვენ მიერ დაწესებული სადაზღვევო მოთხოვნები'), which follows from 'we verify ... insurance' earlier and from the platform not being the carrier. Worth confirming, since the sentence is an exclusion of cover.
- 'data controller' → 'მონაცემთა დამმუშავებელი', the term used in the Georgian Law on Personal Data Protection. Note the false friend for a GDPR-trained reader: in Georgian law 'დამმუშავებელი' is the controller, while the processor is 'უფლებამოსილი პირი' — the opposite mapping to what the English word 'processor' suggests.
- 'grace period' → 'საშეღავათო ვადა'. In Georgian this term is usually used for payment deadlines; here it denotes how long the driver waits at the meeting point. If the lawyer prefers an unmistakable phrase, 'მოცდის ვადა' (waiting period) is available, but it would have to change in both the terms and the cancellation policy together.
- 'any lawful claims period' (driver documents) and 'the service and claims period' (messages) are terse and ambiguous in the English — statutory limitation, or a contractual window for raising a dispute? Both are rendered as 'სასარჩელო ხანდაზმულობის ვადა' (statutory limitation period). A retention schedule generally needs a definite number of years, so this is likely the weakest clause in the privacy notice.
- 'Bookings and financial records: as long as Georgian accounting and tax law requires' is translated as-is, but it names no period. Georgian tax law fixes this term; consider replacing the cross-reference with the actual number of years.
- 'We may suspend an account that abuses drivers or staff' — in English the account does the abusing. Georgian forces an animate subject, so it reads 'ანგარიში, რომლის მფლობელიც ბოროტად ეპყრობა...' ('an account whose holder abuses...'). This is a small substantive shift: it attaches the conduct to the account holder rather than the account.
- Cookies, 'If that ever changes we will ask first' → 'წინასწარ გკითხავთ' (we will ask you beforehand). The English does not say what is asked for or what happens on refusal; the Georgian deliberately does not promise a consent mechanism either. If a consent banner is intended, say so explicitly in both languages.
- Cookies, 'are not shared' → 'არავის ვუზიარებთ' (we share them with no one). The commoner Georgian formula would name third parties ('მესამე პირებს არ გადაეცემა'), which is narrower than the English; the broader wording was kept.
- 'negligence' → 'გაუფრთხილებლობა', the Civil Code term. 'დაუდევრობა' is the everyday word. Note the Georgian, like the English, does not distinguish ordinary from gross negligence — if the exclusion is meant to cover only უხეში გაუფრთხილებლობა, that has to be stated.
- 'identification number' → 'საიდენტიფიკაციო ნომერი' in full in both documents, while the site footer abbreviates it as 'ს/კ'. Left in full for the legal pages; flag if consistency with the footer is preferred.
- 'one-way hashes' → 'ცალმხრივი ჰეში' — a transliteration; there is no established Georgian term, and a non-technical reader will not learn from it that the numbers cannot be recovered. The following clause ('never in readable form' → 'არასოდეს — წასაკითხი სახით') carries that meaning.
- 'a person will answer' → 'პასუხს ცოცხალი ადამიანი გაგცემს' ('a live person'), because a bare 'ადამიანი' would not carry the intended contrast with an automated reply.
- 'a trip that evaporates' is idiomatic and has no Georgian equivalent; rendered literally as a trip that in the end does not take place ('მგზავრობისთვის, რომელიც ბოლოს არ შედგება'). The rhetorical force of the English is reduced.
- Terminology was aligned with the existing Georgian dictionary at C:/Users/user/Claude/RoutePlanner/src/lib/i18n/ka.ts so the legal pages match the rest of the site: ჯავშანი (booking), მგზავრობა (trip), მარშრუტი (route/itinerary), აყვანა/ჩამოსვლა (pickup/drop-off), მხარდაჭერა (support), საკომისიო (commission), 'ლარში იჭრება' (charged in lari), მომსახურების პირობები, კონფიდენციალურობის შეტყობინება, გაუქმების პოლიტიკა.

## Russian

- Interpolation placeholders are preserved verbatim as ${COMPANY}, ${ENTITY}, ${ENTITY_ID}, ${ENTITY_ADDRESS} and ${CONTACT}, in the same sentence positions as the English. IMPORTANT GRAMMATICAL CONSTRAINT: Russian declines nouns, but a runtime-injected string cannot be declined. I have therefore worded every sentence so the injected value can stay in its citation (nominative) form, by putting a governing Russian noun in front of it — 'компания ${ENTITY}' in the terms intro and the privacy controller clause, and 'по адресу: ${ENTITY}, ${ENTITY_ADDRESS}' for the notice address. If the registered name is supplied in a form that already reads as a Russian noun phrase, or if the lawyer prefers the bare name without 'компания', those sentences need rewording, not just a word deleted.
- 'Terms of service' → 'Условия оказания услуг'. I chose this over the more common web-site title 'Пользовательское соглашение' because this document governs the actual booking service, not merely use of the website. If the lawyer wants it to read as a consumer-facing offer/agreement under Georgian law, 'Пользовательское соглашение' or 'Публичная оферта' may be preferred — but 'оферта' carries specific contract-formation consequences the English does not assert, so I did not use it.
- 'Privacy notice' → 'Уведомление о конфиденциальности'. The far more familiar Russian term is 'Политика конфиденциальности' ('privacy policy'). I kept 'уведомление' because the English deliberately says 'notice' and the sibling document is titled 'policy'. Flagging in case Georgian regulatory practice expects the word 'политика'.
- 'Data controller' → 'оператор персональных данных'. This is a genuine terminology conflict and needs a lawyer's decision. 'Оператор' is the Russian Federation's statutory term (152-ФЗ); Russian-language renderings of GDPR usually say 'контролёр'; Russian-language renderings of the Georgian Law on Personal Data Protection sometimes use 'обработчик данных', which in RF usage means the *processor*, i.e. the opposite role. I picked the term a Russian-speaking reader will most reliably understand as 'the party responsible', but the choice should be checked against whatever wording the Georgian regulator's Russian materials use.
- 'Full cover' in 'To change the itinerary with full cover' is ambiguous in the English: it could mean insurance cover specifically, or the whole bundle of protections listed in the previous paragraph (price, support, insurance requirements, our responsibility). I translated it as 'с сохранением полного объёма защиты' — the broader reading, matching the preceding paragraph. If insurance alone was intended, this must become 'с сохранением полного страхового покрытия'.
- 'Grace period' has no clean Russian legal equivalent here. 'Льготный период' is a finance term (payments), so I used 'период ожидания' in the terms and 'период ожидания' in the cancellation policy — literally 'waiting period', which is what it functionally is (the driver waits). This loses the 'we are extending you a grace' nuance but is actionable and unambiguous for the reader.
- 'Caused by negligence' → 'вследствие небрежности'. Georgian/Russian-language civil-law drafting more often uses 'неосторожность' as the umbrella fault category, with 'небрежность' as one form of it. 'Небрежность' is the closer literal match to the common-law 'negligence' this clause is modelled on; if the clause is meant to track a Georgian Civil Code fault standard, the term should be conformed to that code.
- 'Trading name' → 'коммерческое обозначение'. That is the correct civil-law term for a business name that is not the registered name, but it is a specific legal category. If ${COMPANY} is in fact a registered trade mark rather than a trading name, 'товарный знак' would be the accurate word.
- 'We may suspend an account' → 'мы вправе приостановить действие учётной записи'. Russian forces a choice between suspension (temporary) and termination (permanent); I used the temporary reading, which is what 'suspend' says. Note this is weaker than closure/deletion — if the intent is to be able to close accounts permanently, the English needs to say so first.
- 'If that ever changes we will ask first' (cookies) — I rendered this as 'мы сначала спросим вас', keeping the English's vagueness. Russian data-protection drafting would normally say 'запросим ваше согласие' ('request your consent'), which is a stronger and legally specific commitment. I did not upgrade it, because doing so would strengthen the obligation. The lawyer may want to strengthen it deliberately, since consent is what the law will require anyway.
- 'Reviews you leave' and 'quotes you were shown' — 'quote' is rendered throughout as 'ценовое предложение' (price quotation), consistently in all three documents, so the terms clause about accepting a revised quote and the privacy clause about quotes shown clearly refer to the same object.
- 'Mountain routes close' is written in English as a flat statement of fact with no agent. Russian needs an aspect and voice: I used 'Горные маршруты закрываются' (imperfective, habitual — 'mountain routes do close, as a matter of course'), which matches the English intent. It reads as a factual observation, not as a reserved right of ours to close routes.
- 'You are refunded in full' / 'refund it in full' — Russian needs an object, so I wrote 'вернём вам всю уплаченную сумму' ('the whole sum you paid'). This is the natural reading, but note that it makes explicit something the English leaves implicit: the refund is of what the traveller actually paid, which matters where payment is in cash to the driver at the end of the trip and no money has reached us at all. Worth checking that this is intended, and that the cash case is covered somewhere.
- 'Notices in writing go to ${ENTITY}, ${ENTITY_ADDRESS}' — the English does not say whether service at that address is the only valid method or merely one available method. I kept it equally open ('направляйте по адресу'). If this is meant to be the exclusive address for service of legal notice, that has to be stated expressly.
- The word 'мы' in the terms is defined in the intro as ${ENTITY} and used consistently thereafter. In the privacy notice the English never defines 'we' but plainly means the same entity; I did not add a definition, as that would be adding a term. The lawyer may wish to add one there.
